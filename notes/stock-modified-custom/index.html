Stock, modified, custom

We did not set out to build software. We set out to get one test result from the trench to six people without anyone re-typing it, and the stock tools could not do it.

Every shop has lived this with a van. It arrives stock, and the tech spends part of every call digging for a fitting. So somebody screws plywood shelves into the walls, and most of the digging stops. When the digging that is left adds up to a tech you are not billing, you quit adding shelves and build the van to your own drawing, bins labeled by job. Nobody calls that stubborn. The van that gets the tech back on the wrench fastest wins, and when nothing on the lot does, you build one.

Start stock

Information on a job moves through equipment too. Forms. Reports. Approvals. Invoices. The group text. The spreadsheet nobody is allowed to touch. We started where everyone starts, with the conventional version of all of it, and at low volume it held.

It stopped holding at the handoffs. Take a hydrostatic test. One result, and it has to reach the fieldsman, dispatch, the inspector, the insurer, the vendor, and the customer. Six audiences, one fact. With stock equipment that is six times somebody re-keys a pressure and a time, and each re-key is a place for the number to go wrong. The test itself was the fast part. Nothing gets covered until the inspector has the paper, and the trench stayed open while the paper caught up.

Modify for throughput

So we modified. Locked cells instead of free text. Dropdowns where people kept inventing spellings. The fields in the order the inspector asks for them. A naming convention for the shared folder so a report could be found by someone who did not write it. None of that is glamorous, and all of it raised throughput. The same person cleared more reports in a day, with fewer calls back to the field to ask what a smudge meant.

A modified tool is faster at the step it was built for. It still ends where the stock tool ended, at the seam. The report still had to become an invoice. The invoice still had to become a pay record. Every seam was a person retyping. We had sped up the steps and left every seam exactly where it was. That is the limit of modification. It buys time inside a step. It does not change the number of handoffs.

Build at the choke point

When the same seam chokes every week, modifying stops being the cheap option. Building is. So the test report and the invoice are one entry instead of two, because the second was only ever a copy of the first. The lien record looks up the county from the address, so nobody is guessing which courthouse a filing belongs in. The paystub carries the miles reported, so the controller substantiates one record instead of reconciling two.

Each tool on our tools page is a choke point we got tired of.

None of them started as a product. Each started as a place where the work waited on paperwork, often enough that building was cheaper than waiting.

This is not a case against conventional software. It is built for the average shop, and it is good at the average. But no vendor sells the chain from your trench to your invoice to your payroll, because that chain is yours. They sell links. You live in the seams. Modifying a link narrows a seam. Only building closes it.

On a job site, throughput of communication is throughput of work. A test result that reaches six people in seconds instead of a day is a trench that gets covered today. That is the whole case for custom equipment. Not that it is ours. That the choke point is gone.

Start stock. Modify for throughput. Build at the choke point. That is the order Click Construction worked in, and the order we would hand to anyone whose job is still waiting on its own paperwork.
