The 100 decisions before lunch

A superintendent or project manager on a sizable commercial job does not spend the day “managing the schedule.” They spend it making the same categories of decisions, over and over, because the facts those decisions depend on are not in one place.

A normal day breaks down like this.

Field sequencing and manpower: 10 to 30 decisions. Which crew goes where. What gets started. What gets held because another trade is in the way. Whether to pull people off a slow area. None of these are hard in isolation. They get hard when the answer depends on a text from the GC, a delivery that may or may not have landed, and a foreman who is already on the other side of the building.

Constraints and logistics: 5 to 15. Material on site or not. Equipment access. Weather. Inspections. Shutdown windows. After-hours work. A crew can be fully staffed and still produce nothing if one of these is wrong. The decision is usually made late, at the gang box, after the drive.

Coordination with other trades and the GC: 10 to 25. Access conflicts. Who owns a clash. Temporary power or water. Protection of finished work. This is where the day disappears. Each conflict is a small meeting. Ten of them is a lost morning.

Commercial and design: 5 to 20. RFIs that need an answer to keep moving. Pending change direction. A submittal rejection. A pay-app or backcharge dispute. These do not look like field problems until the crew is standing in front of work they are not allowed to finish.

Safety and quality stops: a handful. Any one of them can halt the day. These should stay human decisions. The problem is when a failed inspection or a missing cover arrives as a surprise instead of a known constraint.

Add it up and a quiet day is still 30 to 50 calls that matter. A rough day, with a failed inspection, a missing long-lead item, and two trades in the same ceiling, clears 100 before lunch. Most of those calls exist only because yesterday’s answer never landed with the person who needed it.

Click Construction is built to cut that list down.

Sequencing decisions shrink when the look-ahead only shows work that is clear to run: material on hand, access open, no trade in the way. Manpower moves against that list, not against whoever called last.

Constraint decisions shrink when material, inspections, shutdowns, and deliveries are visible before the crew is dispatched. The question stops being “did it show up?” and becomes “what do we run instead,” which is a real decision, not a scavenger hunt.

Coordination decisions shrink when clashes, access, and temporary services are logged against the task they block, and the update reaches the field and the office in seconds rather than after the next coordination meeting.

Commercial and design decisions shrink when an RFI, a rejected submittal, or an unpriced change sits on the work it holds. Approvals can happen while the question is still small, instead of after it has become a delay and a dispute about who owns it.

Safety and quality stops do not go away. They should not. What changes is that they stop being the way the job finds out it was blocked.

The point is not a quieter phone. It is a shorter list. The person running the work should be making the few calls that change cost, sequence, or risk. Click Construction’s systems take the repeat decisions off that list, so the work that is clear gets installed, and the work that is not clear is named before it burns a day.
