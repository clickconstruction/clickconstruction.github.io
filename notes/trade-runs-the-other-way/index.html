The better trade runs the other way

I came to Click Construction with a Project Management Professional (PMP) certification and a backwards assumption.

Project management, as I had practiced it, was built to bring order to work that does not stand still. Scope, schedule, cost, risk, stakeholders. The certificate trains you to name those things, measure them, and escalate when they drift. Construction looked like the oldest version of the same problem. I thought the useful trade would run from the job site into technology: take what supers and project managers already know about sequencing, constraints, and incomplete information, and teach software teams to respect it.

That was half right. The job site does have lessons software people skip. A task that is not clear to run is not a task. A date with no owner is a wish. A meeting that does not remove a blocker is overhead. Those rules hold in a codebase as much as they hold on a deck.

What I did not expect was how far the other direction runs.

A PMP is trained to control a project by documenting it. Baselines, change logs, status reports, RACI charts, earned value. On a commercial construction job, that stack arrives too late. A superintendent does not lose the day in the monthly report. The day is lost in the 30 to 100 decisions made before lunch: which crew goes where, whether the material is actually on site, who owns a clash, whether an RFI is still open, whether the inspection window is real. Most of those decisions exist only because yesterday’s answer never reached the person who needed it.

The PMP tools assume the information is already gathered, then analyzed. Technology’s useful lesson is the opposite. Do not gather it later. Put the fact where the decision is, at the moment the decision would otherwise be guessed.

That is the reversal. I did not join Click to pour construction discipline into software. I joined and found that the discipline of software, information in one place, updates in seconds, a default that is already correct, is what construction has been missing. The PMP still matters. Percent complete against planned complete, float on the critical path, milestone hit rate, cost against earned work: those are the right questions. They stop being useful when they live in a report the field sees on Friday.

At Click, the same questions sit on the work they describe. An open RFI is attached to the task it blocks. A delivery is visible before the crew is dispatched. A milestone is a date plus the constraint that can still kill it. The person running the job makes fewer decisions, because the ones that only existed to chase information are already answered.

America’s oldest industry does not need less project management. It needs the version that shows up before the crew is standing in the wrong room. That is what I thought I was bringing. It is what the technology turned out to be for.
